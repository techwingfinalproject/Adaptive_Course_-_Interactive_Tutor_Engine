Understanding 3NF with Easy Explanation

Third Normal Form (3NF) eliminates transitive dependencies. A database schema is in 3NF if it satisfies the following two conditions:

  • The relation is already in Second Normal Form (2NF).
  • No non-prime (non-key) attribute is transitively dependent on the primary key.

Formal Definition:

For every non-trivial functional dependency \(X \rightarrow Y\) in R, either:
1. \(X\) is a super key, OR
2. \(Y\) is a prime attribute (part of a candidate key).

Case Study Example

Consider a flat table: Student(StudentID, StudentName, DeptID, DeptName, DeptHead)

Functional Dependencies present:

  • StudentID → StudentName, DeptID
  • DeptID → DeptName, DeptHead

Here, StudentID is the primary key. DeptName and DeptHead are non-key columns. Note that StudentID → DeptID and DeptID → DeptHead. Thus, StudentID transitively determines DeptHead through DeptID. This is a transitive dependency and violates 3NF!

How to decompose to 3NF:

We split the table into two separate tables, separating the transitive relation:

  • StudentTable(StudentID, StudentName, DeptID)
  • DepartmentTable(DeptID, DeptName, DeptHead)

Key Points Summary

  • 2NF vs 3NF: 2NF removes partial dependencies (non-key depending on part of a composite key). 3NF removes transitive dependencies (non-key depending on another non-key).
  • Transitivity: Denoted as \(A \rightarrow B \rightarrow C\), meaning \(A\) determines \(C\) indirectly through \(B\).
  • BCNF comparison: BCNF is stricter. It does NOT allow the second condition of 3NF (which allows \(Y\) to be a prime attribute).
